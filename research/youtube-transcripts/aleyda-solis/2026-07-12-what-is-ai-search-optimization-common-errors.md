## Why this video is in the research set

- This is the opening episode of the AI search roadmap series and the definitional one — it sets out what AI search optimization is, who owns it, and why it sits on top of SEO rather than beside it, which frames the other three episodes.
- Chris Green brings first-party quantitative evidence (indexability as a precondition for chatbot visibility, a PageRank-to-chatbot-crawl correlation, agent behaviour tests) rather than assertion, making it the most empirically grounded episode in the set.
- Lidor Wysocki's account of scaled AI content surging then collapsing on a three-to-six-month lag, and LLM visibility following organic visibility down, is the central cautionary evidence for any AI-assisted content production playbook.
- It contains the most developed measurement discussion of the four: what to warehouse, what proxies work, and why directly attributable revenue is the wrong bar right now.

## Key claims and takeaways

- Aleyda cites her own LinkedIn poll in which 95% of respondents said AI search optimization is owned either by the SEO team or by a dedicated AI search team staffed with SEOs.
- Chris Green ran correlations on his bot data and reports that the most visible URLs in AI chatbots are the indexable ones — if you are not indexable, the chance of appearing in a chatbot result is "virtually zero." He also found chatbot access-log hits correlate with PageRank at roughly a 0.7–0.8 coefficient on the sites he tested, while cautioning it is a small isolated sample.
- Green's agent testing found agents are "super lazy by design": they look at what is indicative of a normal user's experience with the least effort possible, ignore hidden links or elements unless explicitly told to look, and operate under a time budget — so facts belong in the raw HTML, and clever workarounds introduce a brittle layer. On images, he found a chatbot can read an image you upload but otherwise largely misses image content, so valuable text should not live in images.
- Roxana Estingu warns that most LLMs do not render JavaScript the way Google and Bing do, so the server-side raw response must carry the information; some generate a screenshot instead of rendering, meaning content hidden behind accordions and dropdowns is invisible to them — the same advice Google gave for years now applies for a new reason.
- Estingu's prompt-research point: prompts built as extended keywords are wrong, because keywords were always a user's translation of their problem into engine-friendly language, and with ChatGPT users skip that translation and type the problem itself — so prompts should be built from actual customer problems.
- Lidor Wysocki's timing argument: language models pull primarily from Google and Bing databases, and Google spent 2022–2024 introducing policies demoting overly optimized, spammy content. Newcomers to AI search are now building at scale, with AI, exactly the pages Google penalised two years ago — producing a two-to-three-month surge in traffic and citations, then a loss of all of it once detected, typically on a three-to-six-month lag. Her conference presentations over the summer showed that when organic traffic and visibility fall, ChatGPT and the other language models follow the same pattern.
- Wysocki recommends SparkToro for identifying which language models a given audience actually uses, and argues for promoting named real experts from the brand into communities, YouTube and LinkedIn, since models favour user-generated platforms with real conversations and real expertise — an E-E-A-T play that reads as a vote of confidence in the brand.
- Green's measurement position: relative visibility against a competitive set is the best metric currently available, the mindset has to shift back toward PR-style measurement, and anyone over-relying on directly attributable revenue "will never justify the costs relative to the outcome." He notes chatbot adoption grew explosively while revenue did not follow.
- Green's closing tip is to warehouse everything — Search Console, analytics, access log data — and build your own dashboards and correlations rather than waiting for data science teams who are already busy with higher-precedence work, going as deep as correlating price delta in a merchant feed against GA4 conversion rate.
- Estingu's practical proxy: compare LLM-referred traffic against direct traffic for the same pages, since a correlation between them (when nothing else is driving direct) indicates the LLM is also sending unattributed direct traffic. She also notes visibility tools report only whether the brand appears, not in what context, and that harmful context needs to be in the reporting.
- Aleyda cites a SimilarWeb study with Rand Fishkin tracking user behaviour for a week that found a meaningful share of brand recall generated by AI answers converting into direct brand searches, and recommends adding a "how did you find us?" question at the end of the conversion journey to recover attribution that analytics misses.
- Aleyda flags generic sentiment scoring as a trap — a premium brand flagged negative because an answer called it expensive is a context problem, not a sentiment problem — and recommends Gleepy, a free Chrome extension she attributes to John Mueller and Bobbins, for validating structural issues that matter to both AI search and traditional SEO.
