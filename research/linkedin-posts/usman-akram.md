## Post 2 — 11 June 2026 — 26 likes, 3 comments

URL: https://www.linkedin.com/posts/usman-akram5000_seo-aeo-seostrategy-activity-7470800748570906624-kLRl

Rand Fishkin just dropped new data: 68% of Google searches in 2026 end without a click.

Most people will read this and think: traffic is dying, CTR is dropping, we need to find a way to get it back.

Here's what the data actually shows.

In 2016, 45% of searches were zero-click.
By 2024, it was 60%.
Now it's 68%.

That's a 23-point climb in a decade, and the acceleration is speeding up, not slowing down. AI Overviews now appear on 20%+ of all searches and reduce CTR by nearly 60% when they do.

The Ahrefs tracker tells the same story from a different angle: Google traffic sent to tens of thousands of actively-managed websites dropped 22% in the last year alone.

And the users who don't click? They just search again. Google's own data shows a 7-point jump in re-searches, they've built a machine that answers your question and then generates three more.

That's the context. Now here's where most people go wrong.

The question isn't "how do we recover the traffic?" The question is: how do you market in a world where traffic was never going to last?

Scaling content, more programmatic pages, using AI to publish at 10x velocity, none of that is the answer.

That's literally you fighting yesterday's battle.

What actually matters now is what Rand himself has been building toward: measuring influence instead of traffic.

Are people talking about your brand?

Are agents recommending it?

Does your product show up in conversations that happen entirely inside platforms you don't own and can't track in GA4?

That's the new game.

I've made two YouTube videos trying to break this down, what the modern search journey actually looks like and what teams should be doing differently.

You'll find them on my channel, hope they help.

Even more interesting, 68% of CMOs now start with AI before using traditional search engines.

📈 AI search is becoming a dedicated growth channel

📈 Google's role is changing

Google remains heavily used, but increasingly as a validation layer rather than a discovery engine.

The workflow is becoming:

→ AI for discovery

 → Sales for conversion

If your discoverability strategy starts and ends with Google, you're probably missing a growing part of how modern buyers research software.

## Post 5 — 2 June 2026 — 37 likes, 3 comments

URL: https://www.linkedin.com/posts/usman-akram5000_seo-aeo-seostrategy-activity-7467536584251269120-EXRD

It feels like there's a new AI search study every week, with people (myself included) constantly trying to figure out how LLMs work. But let me give you permission to not do that yourself.

In every industry, there are generally two types of people:

→ The people who discover things
→ The people who use those discoveries to make something happen

It's one thing to identify what impacts visibility in AI search.

It's another thing entirely to take those findings and drive meaningful business outcomes with them.

Neither role is more important than the other.

Nikola Tesla helped pioneer electricity, but someone still had to build the infrastructure that brought it into homes and businesses.

The Wright brothers invented the airplane, but entire industries were built by the people who figured out how to scale aviation.

Researchers developed the internet, but companies built products that put it into the hands of billions.

The same logic applies here.

You don't have to spend your time running experiments just because everyone else is.

There are plenty of smart people doing the research already.

Your job can simply be to take those insights, incorporate them into your strategy, and create results.

So if you feel that itch to constantly reverse engineer every AI model, consider this your permission to not scratch it.

You don't have to discover every insight yourself.

You can just use them.

## Post 6 — 21 May 2026 — 39 likes, 19 comments

URL: https://www.linkedin.com/posts/usman-akram5000_googles-own-documentation-called-llmstxt-activity-7463197856812687362-m-9J

Google's own documentation called llms.txt a myth last week. But then...

➜ Their Lighthouse tool launched an audit for it.
➜ At Google I/O, they acknowledged LLMs.txt as a valid directive.
➜ And Google's own dev pages revealed having an llms.txt file

Make it make sense.

Here's the full picture.

On May 15, Google's Search Central team published their AI optimization guide.

In the mythbusting section, they listed llms.txt as something you "don't need" and "can ignore."

They called it an "unnecessary AI text file." Direct quote.

Ten days earlier, Google's Chrome team shipped Lighthouse 13.3 with a new "Agentic Browsing" audit category.

One of the audits checks whether your site has an llms.txt file.

The documentation says without it, agents "may spend more time crawling the site to understand its high-level structure and primary content."

At Google I/O 2026, the Chrome team went further, framing llms.txt as a valid way to give AI agents context about your website.

In a developer keynote. Not a blog post. A keynote.

And then there's the detail that broke out in December: Google's own Gemini API docs at ai(dot)google(dot)dev have an llms.txt file.

This isn't unique to llms.txt.

Google's position on AI search optimization, like their guidance on traditional SEO before it, has never been fully consistent.

They've shifted on content length, structured data, what E-E-A-T actually means in practice.

The advice changes depending on which Googler is speaking, which team published the doc, and what year it is.

That doesn't mean you ignore Google's guidance. You use it directionally.
Read the signals. Do your own analysis.

Make your own judgment calls. The practitioners who've treated Google's word as gospel have always been one policy update away from a bad day.

Shoutout to Crystal Carter for finding this first.
