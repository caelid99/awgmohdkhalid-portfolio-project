## Why this video is in the research set

- It is a multi-practitioner panel (Tom Capper of STAT/Moz, Judith Lewis, Jonathan Moore) describing the actual measurement and goal-setting frameworks they use with paying clients for AI search work in 2026 — not theory, but reporting practice.
- It documents the concrete shift away from rank-and-click attribution toward presence, sentiment and mention-quality metrics, which directly governs how content production is briefed and justified.
- It names the specific tool stack practitioners are using for AI search measurement (prompt trackers, DataForSEO-built custom trackers, YK, SE Ranking, Semrush, Moz/STAT) and the cost thresholds they consider reasonable.
- Jonathan Moore's diagnosis-first strategy model and his explicit red-flag checklist give a reusable gate for deciding whether a content programme (e.g. "200 new articles") is warranted at all.

## Key claims and takeaways

- Judith Lewis says the biggest error is porting keyword rankings into LLM search — she reports on two things only for her LLM clients: money made, and the accuracy of what the AI knows about the brand ("I don't care what fan out queries we're in"), using a tool she calls YK to compare accuracy across Claude versus ChatGPT.
- Tom Capper argues the citation link is no longer the KPI: the mindset should resemble buying print or billboard advertising, because the value is being mentioned, recommended and talked about positively in the answer, with or without a link — and a citation pointing to a third party like the Guardian is arguably more credible, and becomes a digital PR target.
- Capper cites STAT's "pixel depth" metric (how large and how high a result is, rather than its rank) and says it correlates surprisingly poorly with rank — the number one traditional organic result is not even visible the majority of the time, so rank was already failing before AI.
- Jonathan Moore's framework: "good strategy starts with a diagnosis" — strategists diagnose causes rather than leading with the solution. Clients arrive with one of three things: a vision, a symptom (traffic's down), or a proposed solution ("I want to do AI"), and the job is to diagnose the underlying business problem instead of validating the proposed solution.
- Moore stresses separating goals from measures: the goal is what success looks like, measures are the effectiveness of a particular activity, and confusing the two is a primary failure mode. His red-flag checklist is activities before diagnostics, tactics driving strategy, goals confused with measures, and no guiding principles.
- Moore recommends April Dunford's book *Obviously Awesome* and runs positioning and USP exercises with clients before checking whether the external evidence matches how the brand wants to be described.
- Moore says there are over 90 prompt trackers in the marketplace, advises starting with manual checks, and has built his own "Mickey Mouse prompt tracker" on DataForSEO APIs as a cost-effective alternative to expensive tools.
- Capper's prompt-tracking prescription: include some branded prompts in the set, insist on grounded responses rather than in-model-only responses (in-model responses are unrepresentative and yield no citation data), and expect prompt tracking to cost roughly what regular rank tracking costs so the sample can be large enough to overcome localization and personalization noise.
- Aleyda's position is that visible analytics traffic is "the floor, not the ceiling," that 100% accurate prompt tracking is impossible given personalization and prompt dynamism, and that the goal is a representative AI search prompt library covering meaningful topics across customer journey stages, personas, product lines and service lines.
- Capper closes on naming: "you are not ranking in citations, please" — dropping that one phrase alone improves how teams think about the metric. Judith Lewis adds that you must also understand what the person who hired you needs personally, citing a client whose bonus depended on newsletter subscriptions.
