URL: https://www.linkedin.com/posts/jcvangent_google-asks-which-page-is-best-ai-search-activity-7472267647301169152-pj9_

Google asks which page is best.

AI search asks which fact is safest to repeat.

That shift is quietly rewriting how content gets found.

A peer-reviewed study from Princeton and Georgia Tech (KDD 2024) tested what moves AI citations. Adding original statistics won. +41% visibility, the biggest lever they found.

Caveat: that number came from a controlled benchmark, not the live web. Treat it as a direction, not a promise.

The direction holds up. Yext studied 17.2 million AI citations and found pages with first-party data got cited 4.31x more per URL.

The reason is simple. AI repeats what it can verify and attribute. A roundup of other people's points gives it nothing to stand behind. Your own number does.

But it gets complicated. Omniscient Digital analyzed 23,387 citations on branded queries:

- Reviews and social proof: 57%
- Original research and thought leadership: 5.4%

Both are true at once. Original research wins informational queries that build your name. Reviews win branded queries once you have one.

If you run an agency or a lean SaaS team, you already sit on data nobody else has. Win rates. Conversion lifts. Results from last quarter.

Pick one number you own. Attribute it, date it, put it on a page built to be quoted.

One stat like that is harder for AI to skip than 50 blog posts. And your competitors can't copy it.

What's one number from your own work you've never published?

## Post 3 — 11 June 2026 — 9 likes, 0 comments

URL: https://www.linkedin.com/posts/jcvangent_232-clicks-per-1000-searches-reach-the-open-activity-7470822986602811394-k8cV

232 clicks per 1,000 searches reach the open web.

Down from 360 in 2024. That's the new baseline.

Most content strategies are still built for the other 768.

AI summaries are absorbing the rest, and the teams still optimizing for old click rates are building on a number that no longer exists.

So what does the 23% actually look like?

The clicks that survive aren't random. They follow a specific architecture:

- Align (I found what I was looking for)
- Qualify (does this do what I need?)
- Validate (is it any good?)
- Reassure (can I trust them?)
- Convert

Each stage earns the next click. Skip one, and you lose them back to the summary.

When I mapped this with a client recently, we stopped asking "how do we rank?" and started asking: where in the journey does a human still need to click through?

The answer was always bottom-of-funnel, high-specificity pages. Exactly the ones most teams deprioritize.

The brands winning the 23% aren't producing more content. They're producing content that earns the click at the one moment a user decides to leave the AI summary.

That's a positioning problem, not a volume problem.

What does your content architecture look like when you design for the 23%?

## Post 5 — 9 June 2026 — 4 likes, 0 comments

URL: https://www.linkedin.com/posts/jcvangent_getting-cited-in-ai-answers-means-nothing-activity-7470085838752690176-kFQz

Getting cited in AI answers means nothing if the answer doesn't make someone want to act.

Burson analyzed 85 companies across 7 AI platforms and found exactly this. They called it the Credibility Paradox.

Two brands. Same AI platform.

Brand A gets mentioned constantly. But the AI describes them with vague, corporate language. "A leading provider of innovative solutions."

Brand B gets mentioned less. But when it does, the AI says something specific. "Used by 4,000 logistics teams to cut delivery errors by 30%."

Which one drives action?

Burson's data backed this up. Concrete, specific claims scored higher believability than abstract positioning. Every time.

Most teams are tracking the wrong thing. They're counting mentions. They're not asking how those mentions read, or whether the framing actually builds trust.

That's the blind spot.

The metric you want isn't "did we appear in the answer."

It's "did the answer make someone want to find out more."

If your brand shows up in AI responses wrapped in generic positioning, you're not winning visibility. You're just adding noise.

Specific language. Real numbers. Concrete outcomes. That's what scores high on believability.

And believability is what converts.

## Post 9 — 2 June 2026 — 21 likes, 3 comments

URL: https://www.linkedin.com/posts/jcvangent_your-log-files-say-ai-sends-1-of-your-traffic-activity-7467534235294351360-Bq3z

Your log files say AI sends 1% of your traffic.

Semrush's clickstream panel says AI is a waypoint in 80% of your customer journeys.

Both numbers are real. The space between them is where your GEO budget fight is being lost.

Together with Diantha van Surksum, I sat down with Philip J Armstrong (VP Insights and Analytics at Semrush, ex-Nike, ex-Sony) at BrightonSEO last month, right after his "Search is the largest behavioral dataset in marketing" talk. The thing he kept circling, the one nobody wants to print on a deck yet:

Click attribution never actually worked. We just got comfortable relying on it.

The model worked when journeys had two steps. A click. A buy. The moment a journey involved any prior consideration (review sites, comparison pages, a podcast, a friend), the last click stopped being the decision and became the receipt.

Then AI compressed the consideration step into a 90-second answer, the receipt stopped showing the journey, and we noticed.

Three things changed for me in this conversation:

1. Stop defending GEO budget with the referrer number alone. Anchor reporting to a journey signal you can defend (branded search lift, organic-direct ratio, post-citation cohort behaviour). The 1% is supporting evidence, not the story.

2. If you cannot get observed data, run Philip's 20-year-old test. Turn it off. One segment, one geography, four weeks, watch the conversion curve. Marketing mix modelling without the marketing mix modelling budget.

3. The deeper Nike lesson he dropped at the end: "The data was there, it wasn't escalated, and the people who did escalate it weren't trusted." Independent third-party data confirms it. Between 2022 and 2024, Similarweb's clickstream showed Nike's running category bleeding traffic while Hoka grew 59% YoY. Nike noticed in 2024. The data noticed earlier.

AI didn't break attribution. The model we were using was always a proxy. AI just made the gap visible enough that the proxy stopped paying our salaries.
