## Why this video is in the research set

- This is the most directly on-topic episode in the set: three practitioners (Cindy Krum, Gianluca Fiorelli, Celeste Gonzalez) describing how they actually brief, produce and place content for AI search in 2026, including where AI-generated content goes wrong.
- Gianluca Fiorelli's point that the failure mode is in *briefing* the AI (sourcing, instructions, role priming, brand bible) rather than in the generation step is the core production-process insight for an AI-assisted content playbook.
- It contains an explicit citation-led prioritisation workflow from Aleyda: start from where citations actually come from for representative tracked prompts, then diagnose why your existing content is not cited, rather than optimising every page.
- It documents the offsite/multi-format reality of AI citation — YouTube, TikTok, Reddit, reviews, community subdomains — which determines where content investment should go, not just what gets written on the site.

## Key claims and takeaways

- Gianluca Fiorelli says the biggest AI content mistakes are not in commanding AI to write, but in briefing it: what sourcing you give it, what instructions, and what role priming — plus using AI without the brand bible (tone of voice, how the brand addresses users, and specifically the things the brand never says), which produces copy that obviously was not written by someone who knows the brand voice.
- Fiorelli's first named mistake is creating content as if it lives in isolation rather than as part of a bigger cluster on the site, which also means ignoring the buyer/search journey that may start or land on that content.
- Aleyda argues against workarounds — pre-rendering/SSR purely because AI systems do not render JavaScript like Googlebot, or publishing markdown files to get ingested — and against forcing everything into bullet points and tables for chunking; format should follow the purpose of the information and the user experience.
- Cindy Krum's enterprise-client claim: the biggest mistake is focusing only on the website. Onsite work is table stakes, the website is the last part of the journey after people have already learned about the brand elsewhere, and AI systems trust what humans say about you more than what you say about you — so offsite optimisation matters more.
- Krum adds the summarisation argument: an LLM produces a summary drawn from multiple places, so if the only site talking about you is your own, you will not be included.
- Krum reports that YouTube is one of the most cited sources in AI Overviews, and that TikTok's algorithm uses "micro engagement" (what you like, share and comment on) to expose people to brands, so top-of-funnel work has to start where people hang out casually rather than where they search. Celeste Gonzalez confirms from her own behaviour as Gen Z that she searches TikTok first and the website is her last stop.
- Gonzalez's local/enterprise process is review-led: mine review text for potential unique selling points, for what reviewers care about that the brand did not expect, and for gaps between your reviews and competitors' — then make those points prevalent across website, video and social. She uses SparkToro for audience research and built a Chrome extension for Google Business Profile review sentiment analysis, and recommends writing code for sentiment analysis on proprietary customer data.
- Aleyda's prioritisation sequence: check where citations are actually coming from for the prompts that represent your customer journeys per product line, service line and location; expect to find product, help or community pages cited rather than category pages because of the first-hand, user-generated, factual content there; compare competitor cited pages onsite and offsite; and where your content exists but is not cited, diagnose why — over-reliance on JavaScript, poor structure, ambiguous wording, or missing stats and data that would make it the factual, trustable source. She uses the EAT principles as an easy reference for copywriters.
- Fiorelli prescribes tracking the follow-up questions an AI answer nudges at the end, not just whether you appear — "LLMs answer are zero-click to the square" — and looking for repetition across those follow-ups to design the conversational search journey. He also runs a social media "newsroom" to monitor brand and product-name mentions, and favours co-marketing with highly engaged micro-influencers over large ones.
- Aleyda warns against optimising for the default prompts a visibility tool generates, since no tool she has seen asks about your unique selling proposition; she has built a doc, guide and sheet for generating a representative prompt sample. Named tools across the panel: SparkToro (US/UK only per Fiorelli), Yext, Ahrefs, Semrush and SimilarWeb's prompt exports and topic-to-citation grouping.
- Krum's community example: a client's neglected community subdomain that SEO was never allowed to touch "took off" once AI ranking arrived, which she attributes to it being a long-existing community with real humans rather than bots — and she advises linking to, featuring and engaging with off-site mentions of you on YouTube or TikTok to keep that content ranking in both the AIO and the platform algorithms.
