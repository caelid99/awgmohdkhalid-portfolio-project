## Why this video is in the research set

- It is the clearest articulation of Schwartz's 2020-vs-2026 content contrast: he argues AI has not replaced content, only thin listicle content, and describes concretely what replaces it — specific, experiential, mid-funnel content aimed at a user who has already done their top-of-funnel exploration inside an LLM.
- It supplies a budgeting rule directly relevant to B2B SaaS planning: treat LLM visibility as an unmeasurable brand channel, fund it from brand budget rather than cannibalising the SEO line, and do not buy LLM-only tooling.
- It contains Schwartz's "two kinds of SEO goals" model (bottom-line revenue goals versus mushy emotional executive-visibility goals), which is a practical way for in-house SEO leads to categorise AI-visibility requests from executives.
- It includes several first-hand consulting anecdotes about when a B2B company should *not* invest in SEO at all — including a $10k/month IT services client whose entire pipeline came from personal networks — which is a useful counterweight to a playbook that assumes SEO is always the right channel.

## Key claims and takeaways

- Recorded just after Apple announced Siri would be powered by Gemini, Schwartz argues this means Google has won the search wars outright: Google now owns the largest and second-largest distribution platforms, faces no meaningful competition, and can unilaterally decide when to serve AI Overviews, AI Mode, or push users into Gemini. His conclusion is that the industry is restored to where it was three years ago — search is Google.
- Because of that, he advises against buying LLM-only tools, comparing it to throwing budget at a tool that only tracks Bing, and recommends sticking with Semrush, Ahrefs, Conductor and Similarweb.
- He names Meta as the only company that could realistically challenge Google on search distribution — roughly two billion daily users across WhatsApp, Facebook and Instagram, NBA-salary-level AI engineers, and, critically, one of only two genuinely good advertising platforms in existence. He argues OpenAI's monetisation announcements are many years behind Google, Meta and even TikTok.
- On defining the discipline, he takes the broadest possible view: SEO is optimising for search on every surface someone can search on. From a CES media preview he cites glasses, contacts, watches, cars, robots, and his own smart alarm clock (now Gemini-powered) as emerging search surfaces, and predicts AI-enabled pillows will be conversational interfaces.
- The 2020 playbook of listicles sourced from keyword tools is dead because the LLM now generates that listicle itself and interrogates the user (budget, ski versus pool, family versus solo, fly versus drive) until they reach a decision. SEO's place is the middle of the funnel that follows: specific, personal, emotional content about a particular choice, not generic top-of-list content.
- He argues brands should stop chasing head terms and optimise for who the product is for: a company selling women's shoes does not need to rank for "shoes", and one selling toddler shoes does not need to compete with Zappos. He illustrates the failure mode with his early career at The Car Connection, which ranked four-to-six for the term "cars" and got huge traffic until the Panda update — behaviour that he says would never convert in 2026, when the journey ends at a specific used Ford F-150 query.
- His diner-versus-pizza-shop analogy frames the whole argument: businesses that "make it all" make nothing best, and the industry's incentives were wrong because they rewarded ranking on high-volume keywords rather than sales.
- On when not to do SEO: he told a B2B IT company spending $10,000/month on an agency to stop, having discovered on the call that every marquee logo on their homepage (Dell, Coke, HP, government) came from a personal connection — a former employer, a brother who is a VP — and that no CIO would find a tool like theirs via Google. His advice was to spend the money on dinners and conferences instead. He applies a resource-relative test: for a multi-billion-dollar manufacturer whose SEO budget equals one day's factory electricity bill, the spend is trivial and worth it; for a resource-constrained startup it usually is not.
- On measurement, he argues LLM visibility is not measurable and probably never will be unless Google breaks it out in Search Console, which he doubts because it is expensive for them with no upside. Therefore it should be treated as a brand metric, funded from brand budget — analogous to Bay Area AI companies buying billboards to signal they raised money — and never funded by taking dollars out of a working SEO programme.
- He separates two legitimate SEO goals: driving revenue and acquisition, versus "mushy emotional" goals such as an executive who needs the brand to appear when they search on the way to the office. He tells a story about a CEO who insisted on Yahoo rankings and, when Schwartz dismissed it, called his contacts at Yahoo and fixed it himself — the lesson being that unmeasurable executive-visibility goals are still real goals.
- He uses his own paint-matching workflow (photographing a colour, cross-checking Gemini, ChatGPT, Claude and Grok, then buying test paint in-store) to argue the underlying demand does not disappear, only its shape changes — and that the store and paint brand have no idea AI drove that purchase. He notes Google's direct-buying announcement matters more than OpenAI's because Google already has Google Pay, Shopping and years of ads infrastructure.
- He predicts the work becomes harder and therefore practitioners become more valuable: the old bar was "I got traffic", which anyone on Upwork could hit, and the new bar is attributable sales, which cannot be outsourced that way. He is writing a book called *Customer Intelligence* about a framework for identifying your actual users, prompted by how often companies cannot name them — one insurance client insisted they sold to "everyone" until pressed, then landed on women in their 30s in metro areas.
