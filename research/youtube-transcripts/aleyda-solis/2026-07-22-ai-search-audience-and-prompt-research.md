## Why this video is in the research set

- Prompt and audience research is the input layer of the whole AI-search content process — this episode is the one that defines how the prompt library that drives content decisions actually gets built, priced and governed.
- It gives hard numbers on why context-free prompt tracking is unreliable (drift percentages per platform, a cited study on run-to-run variance with and without persona context), which is the evidence base for persona-appended tracking.
- Dan Taylor describes an explicitly ML-flavoured production process — downsampling, evaluation datasets, smoke tests, cheap models as judges, release-candidate prompt sets — which is the most concrete "how it is actually produced" material in the set.
- It covers the operational governance of a prompt library: ownership per prompt, tagging and segmentation up front, and Aleyda's core/experimental/monitoring split.

## Key claims and takeaways

- Andy Chadwick reports pitches where clients tracking 10,000–12,000 keywords want to track the same number of prompts, and calls this a "boil the ocean" approach that adds cost to an already expensive stack for very little ROI, because prompt results are far more volatile than a linear SERP.
- Chadwick's own test: he asked for a half-marathon running shoe recommendation and got different top-two results from a rock-climbing friend asking the same thing, and ChatGPT justified the difference by his CrossFit calf tension versus his friend's heel and Achilles tension. He cites a study where a context-free base prompt changed direction across 25,000 runs with up to 60% difference between runs, dropping to roughly 20% once a persona context was supplied.
- The prescription that follows: append the ICP to every prompt, and run the same prompt once per ICP. If two ICPs return different results and you are visible for one and not the other, that is a concrete gap in how the site targets that audience.
- Myriam's headline rule is "a keyword is not a prompt" — the old search behaviour was conditioned by the technology, and now that scaffolding has fallen off. She adds that prompt language changes which sources appear, that the prompt library or taxonomy is the first step and not the endgame, and that it is a living document.
- She insists on waiting at least 30 days before reading results rather than one week, on validating any trend before tracking it, and on remembering that prompt tracking is "an ideal scenario in a sanitized lab space" — a best case that gives direction, not reality.
- Myriam also flags sentiment bias in prompt wording: asking "can I trust them?" already biases the answer negative, so teams probing for negative stuff should not be surprised by negative sentiment scores. Aleyda extends this with the example of a premium brand whose price positioning gets flagged as negative sentiment when the persona in the prompt is simply the wrong audience.
- Dan Taylor's method: downsampling and evaluation datasets rather than a thousand prompts, optimised smoke tests of 20–30 prompts across libraries with daily iterations to produce a release-candidate prompt set, tracking across ICPs, using cheaper and smaller models as test cases and judges with rubrics of value, then running the surviving candidates on the larger models consumers actually use. He also notes his B2B pharmaceutical clients sit on locked-down corporate machines using Edge, Bing and Copilot, so Copilot matters more to them than ChatGPT or Gemini.
- Chadwick cites monthly citation drift figures (attributed to a Rand Fishkin study): Perplexity 40.5% (steadiest), Copilot 53.4%, ChatGPT 54%, Google AI Overviews 60% (most erratic). On sourcing, Perplexity pulls from live web search and is closest to classic SEO and digital PR, ChatGPT skews to consensus and listicle-type sources, and Google AI Overviews has a 54% overlap with classic organic rankings — so reporting should be per platform, never blended.
- Chadwick's warning on default prompts: tools reverse-engineer prompts from the content and URLs you already have, so a flattering visibility score is a self-fulfilling prophecy.
- Myriam's research inputs before writing any prompts: AlsoAsked or equivalent, the social media team, Google Search Console, Google Analytics, and the traditional marketing team for personas and business objectives — split into historical diagnostics, market intelligence, and latent research (AlsoAsked, Google Trends, social listening). Her prioritisation question for each prompt is whether a bad AI answer here would cost a sale.
- Governance rules: Myriam says if no one is responsible for a prompt, do not put it in — some prompts belong to customer experience or support rather than marketing — and to set up tagging and segments up front, since retrofitting tags via CSV re-upload duplicates prompts and doubles the cost.
- Aleyda's library structure is three-part: core prompts (stable, recurrent, highest-priority journeys), experimental prompts (new products, markets, constraints, audiences, emerging behaviour), and monitoring prompts (reputation, brand representation, risk, compliance) — because AI is now a recommendation engine affecting branding, not only a retrieval system. She recommends starting with a minimum viable list for the single most important audience and product line, and warns that SimilarWeb's prompts-generating-AI-traffic data is bottom of funnel only.
- Dan Taylor's closing tip: harvest Google's own published review sentiment analysis and callouts (for a hotel, that the pool is praised but the restaurant is not) as a baseline for building prompt libraries, rather than guessing what people say about you.
