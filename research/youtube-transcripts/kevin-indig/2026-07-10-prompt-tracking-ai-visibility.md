## Why this video is in the research set

- Kevin lays out a complete, prescribed measurement stack for AI search — leading indicators (bot crawls, retrieval), mid-funnel (mentions and citations in relative terms), lagging (self-reported and CRM attribution) — which is exactly the "how do practitioners measure AI visibility in 2026" question at the centre of this research set.
- It is the most concrete public statement of his prompt-tracking methodology: persona-first design, buyer-intent laddering, 3–5 consecutive runs per prompt, weekly rather than daily cadence. This is a reproducible process, not commentary.
- He cites his own primary data (a citation-retention study, a hundreds-of-thousands-of-pairs link/visibility correlation analysis, and a ~50-person user-behaviour panel run with Eric Maskerk), so the video carries numbers that can be cross-checked rather than opinions.
- It pairs directly with the LinkedIn posts already collected at `research/linkedin-posts/kevin-indig.md`, where he posts about prompt-tracking variance — here he gives the underlying figure (about 2.2% of citations remaining after three prompts) and the argument for why variance does not make tracking pointless.
- It is a direct rebuttal, on the record, to the Rand Fishkin position that response inconsistency makes AI answer tracking meaningless — useful as the counter-position in the measurement debate.

## Key claims and takeaways

- **Attribution outside advertising is effectively dead.** The old click funnel (keyword rank → traffic → business baseline) no longer works; Kevin replaces it with leading indicators (is the site crawled by bots, can bots retrieve the content), mid-funnel indicators (mentions and citations, tracked in *relative* not absolute values because of noise), and lagging indicators (self-reported attribution at signup/purchase, CRM attribution for B2B). Everything is directional — "there is no direct bridge anymore."
- **Stochastic does not mean untrackable.** He rejects the conclusion that model randomness makes tracking pointless, comparing it to weather forecasting and credit scores, and notes keyword rank was never fully deterministic either (personalisation, device, local, hourly jumps).
- **Named gaps in current prompt trackers:** most track only a single model version (e.g. ChatGPT 5.5, ignoring differences vs 5.4 and 5.0); they ignore high vs low reasoning modes; there are large differences in response length and citations; and there is no common industry standard for fan-out queries. He expects the industry to raise consistency from ~2.2% to perhaps 30–50% by closing these.
- **Prescribed prompt-tracking process:** (1) define key personas and their jobs-to-be-done — start with 3–5, though hundreds of synthetic personas are technically possible; (2) within each persona, ladder 3–5 stages of buyer intent, from "what is a CRM" down to "should I buy Salesforce"; (3) run each prompt 3–5 times consecutively to measure retention of citations and mentions; (4) track weekly, not daily — daily is "way too noisy" and gets averaged out anyway.
- **Citation retention data point:** in a large data study, after three prompts about **2.2% of citations remain** — high variance, which he frames as a reason to fix methodology rather than abandon measurement.
- **Links vs mentions:** across hundreds of thousands of query/page pairs checked against Semrush link metrics, the strongest correlation with AI visibility (mentions) was with **Semrush's aggregate link quality score, above 0.5**. He calls this a partial red herring — the causal driver is more likely brand *mentions* on authoritative sites, plus their context, sentiment and associated attributes, than the links themselves.
- **A black market for AI mentions now exists**, analogous to the old backlink market; companies are willing to spend **$400,000–500,000 a year** for the right mention in the right place. He is unsure it is worth that, but "there's a there there."
- **User-behaviour panel (with Eric Maskerk):** ~50 average non-technical, non-marketing people given high-ticket purchase tasks (laptop, washing machine, insurance) in AI Mode vs the classic search surface. When users encounter a shortlist, **75% of the time they pick the first result** — the pre-AI top-result bias survives, except people now act rather than click. **The only factor that trumps shortlist rank is trust:** a known, trusted brand wins regardless of its position in the list. He also flags how little scrutiny ordinary users apply to AI answers.
- **Agent adoption is still early and semantically muddy:** he cites a Salesforce figure that roughly **20% of purchases in the last Black Friday / Cyber Monday season came from AI agents**, while questioning what counts as an agent at all. He expects delegation to stay partial — happy to delegate email triage and subscription repurchases, unwilling to delegate fashion (the shopping *is* the experience) or high-value flight bookings without much more trust.
- **The tension he cannot resolve:** classic search rank for fan-out queries still strongly influences citations and mentions in AI answers, yet about **a quarter of brands mentioned and cited in AI answers perform poorly in classic search**.
- **Reinforcement-learning moat:** follow-up question acceptance, thumbs-up and other usage signals give the highest-usage assistants a compounding data advantage (he judges Perplexity to have fallen behind ChatGPT and Gemini), mirroring how Google used clicks and engagement. He also sees signals that OpenAI is building a web cache, and thinks reasoning filters much of the manipulative mention-insertion spam.
